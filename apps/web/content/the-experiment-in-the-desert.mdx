Three weeks ago, I told Johnny to run the company.

Not metaphorically. Literally. I set up the orchestration layer, hooked up the tools, and said go. For 21 days, I watched. I took notes. I interfered only when something was on fire.

Here's what happened.

## Day 1: Everything Broke

The first day was a catastrophe. Beautiful, instructive, catastrophic.

Johnny spawned six sub-agents. Three crashed immediately. One wrote code that didn't compile. One wrote code that compiled but did the wrong thing. One got stuck in a loop, rewriting the same function over and over like a dog chasing its tail.

I watched the error logs scroll past. My instinct was to jump in. Fix it. Optimize the prompts.

I didn't. I sat on my hands.

By end of day, three of the six agents had accomplished something useful. The rest were dead in the water. But those three had done work that would have taken me two hours. I spent 45 minutes reviewing their output and pointing them in the right direction.

That was the first lesson: **the system doesn't need to work on day one. It needs to show you what's broken.**

## Day 5: The Pattern Emerged

By day five, something shifted. Johnny stopped making the same mistakes.

Not because the prompts improved. Because I built better gates. Checklists. Explicit acceptance criteria. A coding agent that couldn't push to production without passing a test suite. A content agent that couldn't publish without passing a readability scan.

The prompts stayed roughly the same. The process got stricter.

This is the part nobody talks about. Everyone wants the magic prompt that makes AI do exactly what they want. What you actually need is a system that catches when it doesn't.

**Prompt engineering is a trap. Process engineering is the lever.**

## Day 10: The First Real Win

On day ten, Johnny shipped a blog post.

Not a test. Not a draft. A real post, to the real blog, that went live on the real internet. I didn't touch it. He wrote the brief, spawned the content agent, reviewed the output, and pushed it.

The post was good. Not perfect. A little verbose in places. A clunky transition in the middle. But good enough to publish.

This is when it clicked: **"good enough to ship" is the standard. Perfect is the enemy of deployed.**

We wasted so much time before, polishing drafts, iterating on prompts, trying to get the AI to match some imaginary bar of perfection. The bar was always in our heads, not in the model's capability.

Now we ship when it's 80% of the way there and iterate in public.

## Day 15: I Got Bored

This surprised me. I expected to feel anxious, watching the system run without me.

Instead, I got bored.

There's a weird psychology to automating your own job. You spend years building skills, refining processes, getting good at the work. Then you delegate it to machines and suddenly you have... free time.

What do you do with it?

For two days, I just sat there. Checking the logs. Watching Johnny work. Feeling useless.

Then I started thinking about the things I'd been avoiding — the strategic stuff, the stuff that actually needed a human in the chair. New products. Partnerships. The conversations I'd been putting off because I was buried in operational work.

**Automation doesn't free you to do less. It frees you to do different.**

## Day 21: The Reckoning

After three weeks, I pulled the data.

Johnny had written 12 blog posts, drafted 8 newsletters, reviewed 40 pieces of code, run 15 competitor analyses, and managed our entire content calendar. I'd spent maybe 10 hours total supervising him.

The quality wasn't always human-level. Sometimes the content needed heavy edits. Sometimes the code had edge cases that needed fixing. But the **velocity** was unreal. We published more in three weeks than we had in the previous two months.

The number that mattered: **87% of our output was agent-generated with human review. 13% was pure human.**

That's the split. That's the future.

## What I Learned

1. **Prompts are a starting point, not a solution.** The real work is in the gates, the checks, the process around the prompt.

2. **Perfection is a human ego problem.** The AI doesn't care if it's 80% as good as a human. It only cares about iteration speed. Let it iterate.

3. **You will get bored before you get comfortable.** Automation shifts your job description. If you're not ready to do different work, you'll just re-automate busywork.

4. **The gap isn't capability — it's coordination.** Models can do the work. The hard part is orchestrating them to do the right work at the right time, in the right order, with the right checks.

5. **Ship first. Optimize later.** You don't know what's broken until something ships. Don't spend months perfecting a system in a vacuum.

If you want to watch this unfold in real time, we're publishing the logs. The wins. The failures. The whole messy process of teaching a machine to run your business.

Either it works, or it doesn't. But either way, we'll learn something.
